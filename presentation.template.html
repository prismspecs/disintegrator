Losing the
Bearings

A measured run of modelbending — deliberately perturbing the weights of a diffusion model so that it keeps every one of its parts and loses the ability to point them anywhere. Twenty passes, two perturbation shapes, one prompt held fixed.

01

What is being done to the model

Each pass selects a random 1% of the U-Net's values and nudges each selected one by a random amount up to 5% of that tensor's own range. Nothing is removed, nothing is switched off. Then the same prompt is rendered again from the same seed, so every difference you see downstream comes from the weights and nothing else.

def modelbend(module, ratio, max_percent, dist="uniform", generator=None):
    for _, param in module.named_parameters():
        t_range = (param.max() - param.min()).item()
        delta   = max_percent * t_range
        mask    = torch.rand(param.shape, generator=generator) < ratio
        shifts  = draw(dist)                  # uniform(-delta, delta)  or  N(0, delta/√3)
        param[mask] += shifts[mask]

Three words are doing three different jobs here, and keeping them apart is what makes the rest of this legible.

the act
Modelbending

Perturbing a trained network's weights so it produces what it was not built for. One word, compounding like databending, which is itself named after circuit bending.

the effect
Model disorientation

The resulting state. The model still runs completely — every parameter present, every step executed — but the values no longer encode which direction leads toward a coherent image. Nothing is missing; nothing points anywhere.

the frame
Disintegration

What the viewer undergoes across the sequence. Kept for the aesthetic register and the Basinski inheritance, never for the technique itself.

Why not “decay”. Every word in the decay family — disintegration, degradation, corruption, damage — implies loss of integrity: the thing wears away and becomes less than it was. But the parameter count never changes and the magnitudes stay comparable. What is destroyed is alignment. A term on the loss axis quietly files this intervention next to the hallucination it is meant to be distinguished from.

03

The dose is not what the parameter says

Two arithmetic facts govern how this accumulates, and both are easy to get wrong from the parameter names alone.

Coverage compounds. A ratio of 1% does not mean 1% of the model. Each pass draws a fresh mask, so after N passes the fraction touched at least once is 1 − (1 − ratio)^N — 18.2% by pass 20, not 1%.

Drift is a random walk. Each pass adds a fresh zero-mean shift that partly cancels what came before, so displacement grows with √N, not N. Doubling the passes buys 1.41× the drift. The run bears this out exactly: relative drift went 12.78% → 18.07% between passes one and two, a ratio of 1.41.

04

The compass is not a metaphor

At every denoising step the U-Net takes the current noisy latent and returns ε̂, its prediction of the noise present. The sampler subtracts some of it. That prediction is a vector in the same space as the latent — 4×64×64 = 16,384 numbers — and it means: the junk is that way; move the other way. The model's output literally is a direction, and denoising is repeated movement along it.

So the angle between the bent model's prediction and the pristine model's, on identical inputs, is a measurable quantity. That is the disorientation angle θ. Zero means the bent model points exactly where the intact one does. Ninety degrees means its direction carries no information at all about the correct one — and because two random vectors in 16,384 dimensions are almost exactly orthogonal, 90° is the noise floor rather than the midpoint of the scale.

Two rotations, same units

Weight-space rotation is arccos of the global cosine against the pristine weights. Both in degrees, both against the same run.
The run inverted the prediction.

Before measuring, the working assumption in the notes was that weight-space metrics would saturate uselessly near 1.0 while θ swung across a wide range. The opposite happened. The weights rotate substantially — the global cosine falls to —, about — of rotation — while the denoising direction turns only —. The network absorbs an enormous amount of weight displacement into a small change of heading.

Which raises the obvious question: if the heading only shifts by a few degrees, why is the output unrecognizable? Because θ is a per-step error, and the sampler compounds it across twenty-five sequential denoising steps, each one starting from where the last one landed. This is dead reckoning. A few degrees off, held for a long enough run, and you make landfall on a different continent.

05

Uniform against Gaussian

The two runs are matched on everything that could otherwise explain a difference: same bend seed, same image seed, same ratio, same magnitude, same prompt. Only the shape of the perturbation changes, and the two shapes are matched in variance — the Gaussian uses σ = δ/√3 — so what differs is the distribution of damage, not its total.

Uniform is democratic; Gaussian is aristocratic. Under uniform, a shift of 0.9δ is exactly as likely as one of 0.1δ, and nothing ever exceeds δ. The Gaussian tail is unbounded: most shifts are smaller, and a few are far larger than uniform permits. Measured on a test tensor, uniform's largest shift landed at exactly 1.00δ while the Gaussian's reached 2.2δ; at U-Net scale, roughly 43 million draws per pass, the expected extreme is about 3.4δ.

06

Reproducing this

Everything above came off one MacBook — an M2 with 16 GB, running Stable Diffusion 1.5 in fp16 on MPS at roughly 50 seconds per rendered pass. The bend RNG is seeded separately from the diffusion RNG, which is what lets the image seed stay fixed while the bending varies.

python experiment.py --steps 20 --ratio 0.01 --percent 0.05 \
                     --dists uniform,gaussian --seed 42 --bend_seed 7

# or through the CLI, one shape at a time
python disintegrate_sd.py --target unet --steps 20 --dist gaussian --bend_seed 7
